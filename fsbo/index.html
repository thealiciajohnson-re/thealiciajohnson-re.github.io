You can absolutely sell it yourself. Four things decide what it costs you.

Plenty of people sell their own home and do fine. The ones who lose money almost always lose it in the same four places.

I'm a Sonoma County agent. I talk to for-sale-by-owners every week, and I've watched enough of them go to the finish line — some on their own, some not — to know where it goes sideways.

Read these against your own situation. If none of them apply to you, you're in good shape and you should carry on.

The four things that cost you

In the order they usually show up

  1. Pricing from hope instead of from closings

    Almost every FSBO price starts in one of three places: what you paid plus what you put in, what a neighbor says their place is “worth,” or what you need to walk away with. None of those are what a buyer will pay.

    The only number that matters is what has actually closed near you in the last 90 days — and closed prices aren't on the public sites, which is why the estimate you're looking at can be off by a lot in either direction.

  2. Reach — most buyers are working with someone

    The buyer for your house is very likely already working with an agent, and that agent is sending them listings from the MLS every morning. If you're not in that feed, you're not in the running — not because anyone is boycotting you, but because you never showed up on the list.

    A sign and a Zillow post reach the buyers who happen to be looking on their own. That's a real pool. It's just a much smaller one than you think.

  3. Availability — you have a job, buyers look on weekends

    Buyers see homes when they're free: weeknights and weekends, often on a few hours' notice, usually stacking four or five houses into one afternoon.

    If someone can't get in on the day they're out looking, they don't reschedule. They just see the other four. Missed showings never announce themselves — you simply never hear from that person again.

  4. Screening — knowing who can actually close

    Anyone can say they're pre-approved. The expensive version of this is finding out in week three of escrow that the financing was never real, after you've taken the house off the market and started packing.

    The screening happens before the showing, not after the offer: who's the lender, is it an underwritten pre-approval or a soft one, what's the down payment, what has to sell first.

The number nobody runs

Time on market is not free

Say it's a $500,000 house and you're saving 3%. Every month it sits longer than it should costs you roughly five thousand dollars.

Mortgage, taxes, insurance, utilities, upkeep, and the part nobody counts — your own time fielding calls, most of them from agents. Three extra months of sitting can cost more than the commission you set out to save.

That's not an argument for hiring anybody. It's an argument for being honest about the clock, because the clock is where the savings actually go.

If I walk through it in person, I can usually tell you within about thirty seconds which of those four is the one working against you.

That's not a sales pitch, it's pattern recognition — it's what I do all week. And I'd rather you hear the honest version now than three months in.

To be straight with you: I'm not even sure it makes sense for us to work together. That's exactly what the walkthrough is for. If you're better off carrying on alone, I'll tell you that, and I'll tell you what I'd fix before your next open house.
